# Story Ideas for Economics Graphic Novels

These mini-graphic novels tell the stories of people who shaped economics through bold ideas, personal courage, and creative thinking. Each story is designed to inspire high school students to see economics as a living, evolving field that connects to ecology, human behavior, systems thinking, and everyday life.

---

## 1. Adam Smith and the Pin Factory (1776)

**Title:** *The Wealth of Watching*

**Setting:** 18th-century Scotland and England

**Story:** Young Adam Smith, a shy and absent-minded professor, takes a walk through a small pin factory and has a revelation that will reshape the world. By watching workers divide tasks, he discovers the power of specialization. But the story goes deeper — Smith was not a cold calculator. His first book, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, argued that empathy holds society together. The graphic novel shows the tension between self-interest and compassion that Smith wrestled with his entire life.

**Key Concept:** Division of labor, the invisible hand, sympathy as an economic force

**Adversity:** Smith was kidnapped by traveling people as a toddler, lost his father before birth, and struggled socially. He was considered eccentric and forgetful — yet he saw patterns in human behavior that no one else could articulate.

**Inspiration Hook:** Economics starts with observing people. What patterns do you see in your own community?

## 2. Harriet Martineau — Economics for Everyone (1830s)

**Title:** *The Woman Who Translated Wealth*

**Setting:** Victorian England

**Story:** Harriet Martineau grew up nearly deaf and was told women had no place in intellectual life. Undeterred, she wrote a series of wildly popular short stories that explained economics to ordinary people — factory workers, farmers, and families. Her *Illustrations of Political Economy* outsold Charles Dickens. She traveled to America and wrote sharp critiques of slavery as an economic and moral catastrophe.

**Key Concept:** Economic literacy, the economics of slavery, free markets vs. exploitation

**Adversity:** Disability (profound hearing loss from childhood), gender discrimination, poverty after her father's business failed. She supported her entire family through writing.

**Inspiration Hook:** You don't need a degree to change how people think about money and power. You need a story.

---

## 3. W.E.B. Du Bois — The Data Revolutionary (1899)

**Title:** *Every Block, Every Door*

**Setting:** Philadelphia's Seventh Ward, late 1890s

**Story:** W.E.B. Du Bois, a young Black sociologist with a Harvard PhD, is given an impossible task: document the economic life of Black Philadelphia. The establishment expects him to confirm racist stereotypes. Instead, Du Bois conducts the most rigorous urban economic study ever attempted — knocking on every door, interviewing thousands, creating pioneering data visualizations. *The Philadelphia Negro* reveals that poverty is caused by systemic exclusion, not personal failure.

**Key Concept:** Data-driven economics, systemic inequality, the economics of racial exclusion

**Adversity:** Institutional racism, underfunding, academic gatekeeping. Du Bois was denied positions at white universities despite being the most qualified candidate. He built an entirely new methodology because existing economic tools ignored Black communities.

**Inspiration Hook:** Data doesn't lie — but who collects it and how determines what truths get told.

## 4. John Maynard Keynes — The Man Who Predicted the Crash (1919–1936)

**Title:** *The Consequences of Peace*

**Setting:** Post-WWI Versailles, 1930s London

**Story:** Keynes attends the Versailles Peace Conference as a young British Treasury advisor. He watches in horror as the Allies impose crushing economic penalties on Germany. He quits in protest and writes *The Economic Consequences of the Peace*, predicting economic collapse and political extremism. He's mocked — then proven devastatingly right. When the Great Depression hits, Keynes develops a revolutionary idea: governments should spend more during recessions, not less. The establishment calls him dangerous. He changes the world.

**Key Concept:** Aggregate demand, countercyclical spending, the paradox of thrift

**Adversity:** Ridiculed for challenging orthodoxy, health problems (he had a serious heart condition), fought against entrenched economic dogma that demanded austerity during suffering.

**Inspiration Hook:** Sometimes the bravest thing an economist can do is say "the experts are wrong."

## 5. Elinor Ostrom — The Commons Are Not a Tragedy (1960s–2009)

**Title:** *Governing the Commons*

**Setting:** Los Angeles groundwater basins, fishing villages worldwide, Nobel Prize ceremony in Stockholm

**Story:** A young Elinor Ostrom is told she can't study economics because she's a woman. She studies political science instead — and ends up overturning one of economics' most famous ideas. The "Tragedy of the Commons" says shared resources are doomed to destruction. Ostrom spends decades studying real communities — fishers in Maine, farmers in Nepal, forest managers in Japan — and discovers they've been successfully managing shared resources for centuries through cooperation, trust, and local rules. In 2009, she becomes the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in Economics.

**Key Concept:** Common-pool resources, collective action, institutional design, polycentric governance

**Adversity:** Gender discrimination in academia, dismissed by mainstream economists for decades. Her work bridged ecology and economics before interdisciplinary work was valued.

**Inspiration Hook:** The most powerful economic solutions might already exist in your community — someone just needs to study them.

## 6. Muhammad Yunus — Banking on the Poor (1976)

**Title:** *Twenty-Seven Dollars*

**Setting:** Jobra village, Bangladesh, during a devastating famine

**Story:** Professor Muhammad Yunus walks through a village near his university during the 1974 Bangladesh famine. He meets Sufiya Begum, a woman who makes bamboo stools but is trapped in debt to a moneylender — she needs just 22 cents to be free. Yunus lends $27 of his own money to 42 villagers. Every single one pays him back. This experiment becomes Grameen Bank and the microfinance revolution. Yunus proves that the poorest people in the world are creditworthy — the banking system just never bothered to find out.

**Key Concept:** Microfinance, credit access, poverty traps, entrepreneurship

**Adversity:** Fought against traditional banking institutions who called lending to the poor "charity, not economics." Later faced political persecution from the Bangladeshi government.

**Inspiration Hook:** Sometimes the biggest economic innovation starts with the smallest loan.

## 7. Friedrich Hayek and the Knowledge Problem (1940s)

**Title:** *The Map No One Can Draw*

**Setting:** World War II-era London, post-war Vienna

**Story:** Friedrich Hayek watches central planners try to manage wartime economies and sees a fatal flaw: no single person or committee can know enough to run an economy. Prices, he argues, are a communication system — carrying information about scarcity, desire, and cost that no bureaucrat could ever calculate. His ideas are unfashionable during the era of big government, and he spends decades in the intellectual wilderness. But his insight about distributed knowledge becomes foundational to information economics, complexity science, and even the design of the internet.

**Key Concept:** Price signals, distributed knowledge, spontaneous order, the limits of central planning

**Adversity:** Intellectual isolation for decades, overshadowed by Keynes, watched his home country of Austria destroyed by the very political forces he warned about.

**Inspiration Hook:** The economy is a network — and no one sits at the center. What other systems work this way?

## 8. Esther Duflo — The Experimentalist (2000s–2019)

**Title:** *What Actually Works?*

**Setting:** Villages in India, Kenya, and Indonesia; MIT classrooms

**Story:** Esther Duflo is frustrated. Economists argue endlessly about whether foreign aid works, whether school programs help, whether mosquito nets should be free — but nobody runs actual experiments to find out. With her partner Abhijit Banerjee, she pioneers randomized controlled trials in economics, bringing the rigor of medical testing to poverty reduction. Their experiments produce surprising results: free bed nets work better than cheap ones, small nudges change big behaviors, and common sense is often wrong. In 2019, at age 46, Duflo becomes the youngest person and second woman to win the Nobel Prize in Economics.

**Key Concept:** Randomized controlled trials, evidence-based policy, behavioral economics, development economics

**Adversity:** Pushback from economists who considered experiments undignified, resistance from governments uncomfortable with testing their assumptions.

**Inspiration Hook:** Don't argue about what works — test it. Economics is a science, and science needs experiments.

## 9. Thomas Sowell — Economics as Clear Thinking (1970s–present)

**Title:** *Basic Economics*

**Setting:** Harlem childhood, Howard University, University of Chicago

**Story:** Thomas Sowell grows up in poverty in Harlem, drops out of high school, and is drafted into the Marines. After the military, he discovers economics and finds in it a framework for understanding the forces that shaped his own life. Studying under Milton Friedman at the University of Chicago, Sowell develops a gift for cutting through complexity to reveal how incentives, prices, and trade-offs affect real people. He becomes one of the most widely read economists in America, insisting that economics is not about ideology but about following the logic of cause and effect.

**Key Concept:** Incentives, trade-offs, unintended consequences, the role of prices

**Adversity:** Poverty, racial discrimination, dropping out of school. Sowell's journey from Harlem to the heights of academia was driven by intellectual curiosity and disciplined thinking.

**Inspiration Hook:** Economics gives you a superpower: the ability to see past good intentions to actual consequences.

## 10. Wangari Maathai — The Economics of Trees (1977)

**Title:** *The Green Belt*

**Setting:** Rural Kenya, Nairobi, Nobel ceremony in Oslo

**Story:** Wangari Maathai watches the streams of her childhood dry up as forests are cleared for cash crops. Trained as a biologist, she sees what economists missed: environmental destruction is an economic catastrophe for the poor, especially women who walk miles for firewood and water. She founds the Green Belt Movement, paying rural women to plant trees. The movement plants over 51 million trees and becomes a model for ecological economics. Maathai is beaten, jailed, and tear-gassed by the Kenyan government. She wins the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004.

**Key Concept:** Ecological economics, externalities, natural capital, women's economic empowerment

**Adversity:** Imprisoned, beaten by police, divorced by a husband who said she was "too educated, too strong, too successful, too stubborn, and too hard to control." She kept planting.

**Inspiration Hook:** The economy and the environment are not separate systems. What happens when you treat nature as capital?

## 11. Amartya Sen — Freedom as Development (1943–present)

**Title:** *The Boy Who Saw the Famine*

**Setting:** Bengal, 1943; Cambridge and Harvard

**Story:** Nine-year-old Amartya Sen watches people die in the Bengal Famine of 1943. There is enough food — but the wrong people have it. This childhood trauma drives him to study economics, where he makes a revolutionary discovery: famines are not caused by lack of food but by lack of freedom and entitlements. People starve when they lose the ability to buy food, not when food disappears. Sen develops the "capabilities approach" — arguing that development should be measured not by GDP but by what people are actually able to do and be.

**Key Concept:** Capabilities approach, entitlement theory, development as freedom, famine economics

**Adversity:** Survived the Bengal Famine, oral cancer as a young student (given a 15% survival rate), challenged the dominant GDP-focused model of development.

**Inspiration Hook:** What if the economy's job isn't to make money, but to make freedom?

## 12. Janet Yellen — Steady Hands in Crisis (2008–present)

**Title:** *The Calmest Person in the Room*

**Setting:** The Federal Reserve, Washington D.C., during the 2008 financial crisis

**Story:** Janet Yellen has spent her career studying why people lose jobs and what happens to families when they do. When the 2008 financial crisis hits, she is one of the few Fed officials who saw it coming. She pushes for aggressive action to prevent a second Great Depression. Rising through the ranks, she becomes the first woman to chair the Federal Reserve and later the first woman to serve as U.S. Secretary of the Treasury. Her story is about the power of patience, data, and caring about unemployment as a human crisis, not just a statistic.

**Key Concept:** Monetary policy, unemployment, financial crises, central banking

**Adversity:** Gender barriers in a male-dominated field, pressure from political forces on both sides, navigating the most severe economic crisis in 80 years.

**Inspiration Hook:** Behind every interest rate decision is a question: how many people will have jobs tomorrow?

## 13. Ibn Khaldun — The First Economist (1377)

**Title:** *The Muqaddimah*

**Setting:** 14th-century North Africa and Al-Andalus

**Story:** Four centuries before Adam Smith, a North African scholar named Ibn Khaldun is writing the most advanced economic theory the world has ever seen. Exiled from court after court, surviving plagues that kill his family, shipwrecks, and political intrigue, he retreats to a castle in Algeria and writes *The Muqaddimah* — a masterwork that describes supply and demand, the labor theory of value, the dangers of excessive taxation, and the rise and fall of civilizations as economic cycles. His work is largely forgotten by European economists for 500 years.

**Key Concept:** Supply and demand (centuries before Marshall), taxation theory, economic cycles, the economics of civilization

**Adversity:** Lost his family to plague, exiled repeatedly, his contributions were ignored by the Western canon for centuries.

**Inspiration Hook:** The history of economics is bigger and older than any one culture. Who else has been left out of the story?

## 14. Lisa Cook — Counting What Was Lost (2010s)

**Title:** *The Innovation Gap*

**Setting:** Jim Crow America, modern research universities, the Federal Reserve Board

**Story:** Economist Lisa Cook asks a question no one had quantified before: what did racial violence cost the American economy in lost innovation? By studying patent data, she discovers that lynchings and racial terror caused a measurable, devastating drop in Black invention and entrepreneurship that lasted for decades. Her research puts a number on the economic cost of racism — not as a moral argument, but as hard data. In 2022, she becomes one of the first Black women to serve on the Federal Reserve Board of Governors.

**Key Concept:** Innovation economics, the cost of discrimination, patent data as economic evidence, institutional barriers

**Adversity:** Resistance from economists who questioned whether racism could be measured economically, long struggle for academic recognition.

**Inspiration Hook:** Every barrier to participation is a tax on innovation. What ideas is the world missing right now?

## 15. Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson — Why Nations Fail (2012)

**Title:** *The Border at Nogales*

**Setting:** Nogales, Arizona / Nogales, Sonora — the same city split by a border

**Story:** Stand at the border fence in Nogales and look both ways. Same geography, same climate, same people — but radically different levels of prosperity. Why? Acemoglu (a Turkish-Armenian immigrant) and Robinson argue it comes down to institutions: inclusive institutions that spread power and opportunity create wealth; extractive institutions that concentrate power create poverty. Their work draws on centuries of history, from the Roman Empire to the colonization of the Americas to modern-day Zimbabwe, showing that economics is ultimately about the rules of the game.

**Key Concept:** Institutional economics, inclusive vs. extractive institutions, why geography and culture are not destiny

**Adversity:** Acemoglu fled political instability in Turkey, challenged comfortable narratives about why some countries are rich and others poor.

**Inspiration Hook:** The rules of the game matter more than the resources on the field. Who writes the rules in your community?

## How to Use These Stories

Each story can be developed into a **4–8 page mini-graphic novel** with:

- **Opening panel:** A vivid scene that hooks the reader into the time and place
- **The question:** What problem or observation drove this person's work?
- **The struggle:** What obstacles — personal, institutional, intellectual — did they overcome?
- **The breakthrough:** The key insight, shown visually (a graph, a moment, an experiment)
- **The legacy:** How their idea connects to students' lives today
- **Discussion prompt:** A question that invites students to apply the concept
